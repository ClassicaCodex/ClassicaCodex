Ptolemy's Harmony

Greek harmonic science, on the instrument it was discovered on. Every interval here is synthesised from the exact whole-number ratio its author wrote — Archytas in the fourth century BC, Eratosthenes, Didymus, and Ptolemy at Alexandria around AD 150, in the Harmonics, the companion to his Almagest.

This page makes sound. Nothing will play until you press the button, and nothing is downloaded when you do — every note is built from its ratio as it sounds. If you are somewhere quiet, the ratios, the table and the monochord all work in silence.

The monochord

One string, and a bridge you can move. Drag it. The pitch rises as the sounding length falls — the octave is half the string, the fifth two thirds, the fourth three quarters — and that inverse is the whole of the discovery: a musical interval is a ratio of two small whole numbers, and you can see it on a ruler.

1:1 the open string

Hold the open string and drag slowly through the fifth. A little either side of it the two notes beat against each other, a wobble you can count; at exactly 3:2 the beating stops and the pair locks. That is what a just interval is, and it is why the Greeks trusted small numbers rather than ears alone.

The divisions of the tetrachord

A tetrachord spans a perfect fourth, exactly 4:3. Its outer notes are fixed and its two inner ones move, and where they are put is what makes a genus. Every division below multiplies out to the fourth exactly — 498.0 cents — with nothing rounded and nothing left over.

Two labels are computed rather than asserted. pyknon means the two lower intervals together are smaller than the top one, which is what actually separates the enharmonic and chromatic genera from the diatonic. epimoric means every ratio in the division is superparticular, of the form (n+1):n — the form Ptolemy insists on. Watch which of his eight lacks it.

division intervals, low to high cents

The enharmonic is the one to hear. Its lowest two steps are a pair of quarter-tones — 46:45 and 24:23, 38 and 74 cents — crowded at the bottom under a wide major third. It sounds nothing like a scale, and Greek writers were already complaining by Ptolemy's day that singers could no longer manage it.

The same three ratios, in a different order

Didymus and Ptolemy divide the fourth with the identical three intervals — 16:15, 9:8, 10:9. Only the order differs: the tone and the minor tone change places. It is the most confusable pair in Greek harmonics, and the difference is audible, because the middle note moves by exactly a syntonic comma, 81:80, a fifth of a semitone.

The two middle notes in turn over a held open string, then both at once. The second time they sound together you are hearing 21.5 cents of disagreement as a slow beat — the same comma that separates a just major third from a Pythagorean ditone, and the reason tuning has never had one right answer.

The small intervals

What is left over when two routes to the same note miss each other. A comma is a discrepancy and is tiny; the limma and apotome are not commas at all but the two unequal halves the tone divides into — and they differ from one another by a Pythagorean comma exactly.

Where the numbers come from

  • Ptolemy, Harmonics I.15–16 and II.14, for his eight genera and for the divisions of Archytas, Eratosthenes and Didymus that he reports.
  • John Chalmers, Divisions of the Tetrachord (Frog Peak, 1993) is the standard modern catalogue; chapter 2 covers the arithmetic tradition and chapter 9 is the catalog itself.
  • The ratios on this page were taken from two independent reference tables that agree completely, and every one of them is checked to span exactly 4:3 before the page is built. A mistyped ratio fails that check; it would not fail the ear, which is the reason for checking. 106 tests, 1 skipped.
  • One row is incomplete on purpose. The Pythagorean enharmonic used the ditone 81:64 over a pyknon of 256:243, but how that pyknon was divided is not recorded. It is shown undivided and cannot be played, because inventing a split would make it the only false line in the table.
